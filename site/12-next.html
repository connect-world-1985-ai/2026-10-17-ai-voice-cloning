1. Run the bake-off on your own voice

  • Why first: every leaderboard on this site uses other people's voices, and the main independent board is English-only. Similarity to you, and Mandarin quality for your voice, can only be measured with your recordings. Inference
  • Decision needed: which 3–4 tools, and whether you are willing to upload 1–2 minutes of your voice to one or two cloud vendors (read their licences on page 9 first).
  • What we would do: follow the experiment on page 11: same three paragraphs (English, Mandarin, mixed technical), same reference clip, automatic scores plus a blind A/B session.
  • What we learn: a ranked shortlist backed by your ears, plus real per-chunk failure rates for your technical vocabulary.

2. Settle commercial use and the vendor licence

  • Why: "maybe commercial" keeps every option open but makes the open-weight landscape murky; several strong models have non-commercial or revenue-limited weights (page 3). On the cloud side, the question is whether you accept a perpetual licence to your voice (ElevenLabs, Cartesia, Fish) or prefer a vendor that does not train on it. Verified (licence texts)
  • Decision needed: is this personal, employer, or your own paid product? Who owns the content? Does a perpetual vendor licence to your voice bother you?
  • What we would do: a one-page decision record per tool (licence, terms clause, risk), and, if it is company content, a short agreement draft for you and your employer on scope, deletion and who may generate with the clone.

3. Build the pipeline for one real lesson

  • Why: the gap between a nice demo clip and a 15-minute lesson with slides, captions and consistent pacing is where most of the work is (page 8).
  • Decision needed: which lesson (ideally one with technical terms, numbers and some Mandarin), which slide tool (Marp, reveal.js, PowerPoint export), and which TTS from the bake-off.
  • What we would do: give a coding agent the prompt on page 11, then review the QA report together: failure rate per check, time spent listening, cost per finished minute.
  • What we learn: real numbers for "human minutes per finished minute", which decides whether a 40-lesson course is realistic.

4. Solve the cross-lingual question

  • Why: you want your voice in the language you have recorded less of. Vendors disagree on how well this works (ElevenLabs' v4 launch claims a native accent; its own older PVC guide warns the source accent carries over; Google Chirp 3 cannot transfer English to Chinese at all). No independent cross-lingual similarity benchmark was found. Verified (vendor docs) · benchmark gap Unverified
  • Decision needed: which accent you want when "you" speak the other language: your own natural accent, or a native one.
  • What we would do: three reference conditions per tool (English reference, Mandarin reference, a mixed reference), scored by a native listener for accent and by SIM for identity.
  • Also worth testing: record yourself reading the target-language script as well as you can, then use voice conversion (page 4) to fix only the timbre, keeping your real prosody.

5. Decide on hardware: buy, rent or cloud-only

  • Why: your current PC has an Intel UHD integrated GPU and 64 GB RAM. That runs CPU-only models slowly; GPU-hungry models will not be practical. Inference
  • Options: (a) cloud TTS only; (b) rent a GPU by the hour for batch rendering (on 2026-10-17 RunPod listed an RTX 3090 at $0.50/h and an RTX 4090 at $0.89/h) Verified; (c) buy a 16–24 GB NVIDIA card.
  • Decision needed: only after the bake-off. If a cloud tool wins on quality, hardware is irrelevant; if a local model wins, the cost calculator shows how many hours of narration make buying worthwhile.
Smaller questions to keep in mind
  • Do you want a lip-synced avatar of yourself, or are slides plus voice enough (page 8)?
  • Which platforms will publish the course? That decides disclosure wording (Udemy's exact sentence, Chinese labels; page 9).
  • Should old lessons ever be regenerated when a better model appears, or frozen as published?
