Measuring quality and controlling the output

"Sounds good to me" does not scale to a 40-lesson course. This page covers the three things you can measure (does it sound like me, did it say the right words, does it sound natural), the ways synthetic speech typically fails, and the controls you have over each failure.

Bottom line
  • Two automatic checks catch most real problems: ASR word/character error rate against your script, and a duration sanity check. Run both on every chunk. Practice
  • Speaker similarity scores only mean something relative to your own real-versus-real baseline, measured with the same embedding model. Verified (Seed-TTS paper; scales differ between models)
  • Pronunciation of technical terms is the most frequent annoyance in teaching content; fix it once in a lexicon and keep a separate spoken script. Practice
  • Whether it sounds like you, and whether the emphasis is right, still needs ears. Use a blind A/B test when you change tools or settings (page 11).

The three measurable questions

1 · Does it sound like me?

Speaker similarity (SIM): a speaker-verification model turns each clip into a vector; SIM is the cosine of the two vectors. Measures timbre and identity only.

2 · Did it say the right words?

Intelligibility: transcribe the generated audio with ASR and compare to the script. WER (word error rate) for English, CER (character error rate) for Mandarin.

3 · Does it sound natural?

Naturalness / preference: human ratings (MOS, CMOS, A/B), or an automatic MOS predictor as a cheap triage signal.

  1. Measure your baseline once. Take 10 pairs of your own real recordings (different sentences, same setup). Baseline B = mean SIM of those pairs.
  2. Score every generated chunk against your golden reference clip with the same model.
  3. Flag a chunk if SIM < B − 0.10, and plot the per-lesson mean to catch slow drift. Inference (a suggested rule, not a published standard)
What SIM cannot tell you SIM rewards matching the microphone and room as well as the voice. It is blind to mispronunciation, wrong tone, and flat delivery. A clip that copies noise from a bad reference can score high. Clips shorter than 2–3 s give noisy scores. Practice

Normalise both sides before scoring, otherwise "3" versus "three", punctuation and casing inflate the error rate:

  • English: Whisper's own EnglishTextNormalizer, then jiwer.wer.
  • Mandarin: remove punctuation and spaces, convert full-width to half-width, map digits the same way on both sides (WeTextProcessing), then compute CER character by character.
Suggested gate per chunk Regenerate if, after normalisation: EN WER > 5 %, or ZH CER > 3 %, or any protected term (product names, code identifiers, numbers) is missing, or the ASR shows more than 2 inserted words (hallucinated speech). Inference (my thresholds, about 2× the human error rate above; tune on your own material)

Caveats. One wrong word in a 10-word sentence is already 10 % WER, so short chunks are jumpy. Whisper itself hallucinates on silence and "corrects" disfluencies, and it fails on rare technical terms, causing false alarms. Keep a whitelist of known terms, or check them via forced-alignment confidence instead (page 8). Practice

The cheapest check: duration

Expected duration ≈ words ÷ your speaking rate (about 150 words per minute in English, about 4–5 characters per second in Mandarin, both rough). A chunk whose real duration is below 0.7× or above 1.4× of expected almost always has a skipped sentence, a loop or a long silence. This costs nothing and runs before ASR. Inference

Automatic MOS predictors (triage only)

  • UTMOSv2 (MIT, sarulab-speech): VoiceMOS 2024 Track 1 (high-quality synthetic speech) first in 7 of 16 metrics, second in the rest. Output is not deterministic by default. Verified
  • UTMOS22 (MIT): the "UTMOS" score cited in most TTS papers. Verified
  • DNSMOS (Microsoft): built for noise suppression; good at detecting noise and artefacts, weak on prosody. Practice
  • NISQA-TTS: code MIT but weights CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so non-commercial. Verified

These predictors correlate with listener panels on average, not clip by clip. Use them to sort chunks for listening, not to accept or reject automatically. Practice

Controls, by vendor and model

  • No SSML <break> on Eleven v4 or v3. Docs: "Eleven v4 and Eleven v3 do not support SSML break tags". Use punctuation, ellipses and audio tags. Older models accept <break time="1.5s"/> up to 3 s, but too many cause instability. Verified
  • Inline IPA on v4: write the pronunciation between slashes in plain text, e.g. /ˈkuːbərˈnɛtiːz/. Verified
  • Pronunciation dictionaries: upload a PLS or TXT file, or create one from rules via the API (alias or phoneme rules; case-sensitive by default). Up to 3 dictionaries per request, each versioned (version_id). Phoneme rules work only on eleven_v4, eleven_v3 and eleven_flash_v2; other models use alias rules only. Verified
  • Audio tags in square brackets on v3/v4: [whispers], [excitedly], [sighs]. v4 follows them more reliably, but tags can be misread as sound-effect requests. Professional clones are "not fully optimized for Eleven v3"; use v4. Verified
  • Voice settings: stability 0.5 (higher = more monotone), similarity_boost 0.75, style 0, speed 1.0. Verified (API reference defaults)
  • Continuity: previous_text/next_text, or up to 3 previous_request_ids/next_request_ids (≤ 2 hours old; not on v3; IDs override text). seed is "best effort", not guaranteed. Verified
  • Normalisation: apply_text_normalization = auto/on/off; language_code forces the language. Verified

Minor conflict: one ElevenLabs page says SSML phoneme tags work only on eleven_flash_v2, a blog post says Flash v2 and Turbo v2 in English. Irrelevant if you use v4 with dictionaries.

  • The most complete SSML stack: <phoneme alphabet="ipa|sapi|ups">, <sub>, <break>, <prosody>, <lexicon uri="…">. Verified
  • Custom lexicon = W3C PLS 1.0 XML at a public or SAS URL; one locale per file; ≤ 100 KB; cached by URI for up to 15 minutes; a SAPI phone set exists for zh-CN. Not supported by the Long Audio API (use batch synthesis). Verified
  • WordBoundary events give word, punctuation and sentence offsets (in 100-ns ticks), ready for captions. Verified
  • Classic Cloud TTS voices: SSML with <phoneme alphabet="ipa|x-sampa">; for Chinese alphabet="pinyin" (Cantonese jyutping); custom pronunciations per request. Verified
  • Chirp 3 HD: Google's own pages contradict each other: one says SSML is supported in Preview for synchronous requests, the voice list says Chirp 3 HD "doesn't support SSML input". Test before relying on it. Unverified
  • Gemini TTS is steered by natural-language style prompts and inline vocal events rather than SSML. Verified
  • IndexTTS2: mix characters and pinyin with tone numbers, e.g. 打DI1去银行 (only syllables in its pinyin vocabulary). IndexTTS2.5 adds per-character replacement <行|HANG2> and English CMU phonemes <minute|M IH1 . N AH0 T>; emotion vectors (emo_alpha ≈ 0.6 or lower) and a duration factor. Verified (README)
  • Fun-CosyVoice 3: "pronunciation inpainting" with pinyin and CMU phonemes, e.g. 对报道[j][ǐ]予好评; tags [breath], [laughter], <strong>…</strong>; natural-language instructions for dialect, speed and emotion. Verified (README, example.py)
  • GPT-SoVITS: Chinese front end uses the G2PW model for polyphone disambiguation; a direct pinyin-override syntax was not found. Unverified
  • F5-TTS and similar flow models need the reference clip's exact transcript; a wrong transcript degrades output. Practice
  • pypinyin (MIT) helps you generate and check pinyin for your override lists.

Text normalisation: the spoken script

Keep two versions of every script:

Display script
What appears on slides and in captions: kubectl get pods -n prod, "v2.10", "¥1,500".
Spoken script
What the TTS receives: "kube control get pods, dash n, prod", "version two point ten", "一千五百元".

The agent creates the spoken script with rules plus a project lexicon, and reviews new lexicon entries with you once. Libraries: WeTextProcessing (zh + en, Apache-2.0), NeMo text processing (Apache-2.0), num2words (LGPL-2.1), or an LLM pass with explicit rules (ElevenLabs recommends this). Verified (licences)

Chunking, reference clips and seeds

  • Chunk at sentence or paragraph boundaries, 1–3 sentences (about 100–400 characters). Never split inside a sentence, and never across a slide marker. Practice
  • One golden reference clip per language for zero-shot models: 5–15 s, clean, no music or reverb, recorded on your normal mic, in the lecture tone you want, ending on a complete sentence, with an exact transcript. Pass the same clip to every chunk. Practice
  • Seeds: derive the seed from a hash of the chunk text and settings so re-runs reproduce; to fix a bad chunk, change only the seed. Lower temperature or top-p is more stable but flatter. Practice
  • Cross-lingual: if your Mandarin reference is better than your English one (or the reverse), test both as references for each language. The accent carries over from the reference to a varying degree per model (page 1). Inference
